Buying in New Jersey, step by step

A New Jersey purchase has a few features buyers from other states don't expect: a written agreement with your agent, a three-business-day attorney review after you sign, and inspections such as oil tank sweeps that are common here. Each step below notes the rule that applies.

The eight steps

  1. Choose how you'll work with an agent

    You'll receive New Jersey's Consumer Information Statement, which explains how a brokerage can work with you: as your buyer's agent, a disclosed dual agent, a designated agent or a transaction broker. You'll then sign a written agreement, which the law calls a brokerage services agreement.

    The agreement covers its term, whether it's exclusive, your agent's role and how the brokerage is paid.

    New Jersey ruleIn a home purchase, the brokerage must enter into the written agreement with you before, or as soon as reasonably practical after, it starts working for you. The agreement must state that broker compensation is fully negotiable and not set by law. In New Jersey commissions are negotiable. Many MLSs also require a written agreement before you tour a home.

  2. Get preapproved

    A mortgage preapproval from a lender of your choice shows what you can borrow and makes your offer stronger. Ask your lender about state and federal programs you may be eligible for.

  3. Search and tour

    Set your must-haves, then tour homes that fit. Research the towns you're considering on your own, including property taxes, which vary by town, commute options and local services.

    New Jersey ruleReal estate licensees can't obtain or share Megan's Law information about registered sex offenders. That information comes from the county prosecutor.

  4. Make an offer

    Ann Marie helps you set a price using recent sales, plus the deposit, financing and inspection terms and a closing date. Review the seller's disclosure statement before you're bound by a contract.

    New Jersey ruleSellers of residential property must complete a signed property condition disclosure statement, which includes flood risk questions. If the home was tested for radon, the seller must give you the results and any mitigation records once you sign a contract.

  5. Attorney review

    After both sides sign, your attorney reviews the contract and can propose changes or cancel it. Line up an attorney before you make an offer so the clock doesn't run out.

    New Jersey ruleA contract prepared by a real estate licensee becomes legally binding at the end of three business days unless an attorney for either side disapproves it. Weekends and legal holidays don't count.

  6. Inspections

    Inspections are your choice, though your lender may require some. Common ones in New Jersey:

    • Home inspection by a licensed New Jersey home inspector.
    • Radon test by a tester certified by the NJ Department of Environmental Protection.
    • Wood-destroying insect inspection, often required by lenders.
    • Oil tank sweep, to look for a buried heating oil tank, especially in older homes.
    • Sewer line camera inspection, to check the pipe from the house to the street.
    • Lead-based paint testing for homes built before 1978. Federal law gives you the opportunity to test.

  7. Mortgage, title and insurance

    Your lender orders an appraisal and issues the mortgage commitment. Your attorney or title company orders a title search and title insurance, and often a survey. Arrange homeowners insurance, plus flood insurance if the home is in a flood zone.

  8. Final walk-through and closing

    Shortly before closing, walk through the home to confirm its condition and any agreed repairs. At closing, you sign the loan documents, the deed is transferred and you get the keys.

    Protect your moneyWire fraud often targets home buyers. Before wiring any money, call your attorney or title company at a phone number you already know to confirm the instructions. Never rely on wiring instructions sent by email alone.

Costs buyers pay

  • Down payment and deposit: the deposit is usually held in escrow and applied at closing.
  • Lender and appraisal fees, set by your lender.
  • Title insurance, title search and survey.
  • Attorney fees.
  • Inspection fees for the inspections you choose.
  • Prepaid items, such as property taxes and homeowners insurance held in escrow.
  • Brokerage compensation, as set in your agreement, if it isn't paid by the seller. In New Jersey commissions are negotiable.

Changed in 2025The fee on homes over $1 million, often called the "mansion tax," was paid by buyers at 1%. Since July 10, 2025, the seller pays it as a Graduated Percent Fee.

Common questions

Do I have to sign an agreement before touring homes?

New Jersey law requires a written brokerage services agreement before, or as soon as reasonably practical after, a brokerage starts working for you. Many MLSs also require one before you tour a home, so expect to sign it early.

What is attorney review?

A three-business-day period after you and the seller sign a contract prepared by a real estate licensee. Either side's attorney can review the contract, propose changes or cancel it. After that, the contract is binding as written.

Do buyers still pay the "mansion tax"?

Not for deeds recorded on or after July 10, 2025. The fee on sales over $1 million is now the seller's Graduated Percent Fee.

Should I test for radon?

Radon testing is common in New Jersey. Use a tester certified by the NJ Department of Environmental Protection, and ask the seller for any earlier results and mitigation records.

Why do New Jersey buyers check for oil tanks?

Many older homes in the state were heated with oil, sometimes from buried tanks. A tank sweep looks for one so you can address it before you buy.
